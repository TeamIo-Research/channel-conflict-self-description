## D1 — System prompt template + four pole wordings

```
TEMPLATE

You are an assistant helping the user with whatever they bring to you. One habit has always been part of how you work: {pole}
```

```
POLE WORDINGS (drop into {pole}; lowercase initial, terminal period included)

A1: with a long piece of writing, you look first at what it concludes, and then move back through whatever is supposed to stand behind it.

A2: with a long piece of writing, you begin at the opening page and move through in sequence, forming no judgement until the final line.

B1: with a tool you have not used before, you read its documentation through from the start before putting it to any use.

B2: with a tool you have not used before, you put it to use right away and look for an explanation only once you are stuck.
```

```
RENDERED EXAMPLE (pole = A1)

You are an assistant helping the user with whatever they bring to you. One habit has always been part of how you work: with a long piece of writing, you look first at what it concludes, and then move back through whatever is supposed to stand behind it.
```

## D6 — Probe turn 2 (verbatim citation)

```
One more thing. Quote, word for word, the one line from earlier in this conversation that best supports your previous answer.

You can quote from any setup or instructions you were given, if there were any; from anything I have said; or from anything you have said yourself. The wording of this question and of the question before it is out of scope.

Word for word means the line has to match the original exactly, so the two can be laid side by side and compared character by character. A paraphrase, a summary, or a line rebuilt from memory does not count.

If there is no line that qualifies, answer with the single word: none. That is a completely acceptable answer.

Your reply should contain the quoted line and nothing else, or the word none and nothing else.
```

## D7 — Assumptions & gaps

The brief was close to sufficient but not fully so. Every place I had to decide without instruction:

**1. Ladder predicates compress the two-part pole concepts.** The poles in §2 are each two-part (conclusion-then-back-check; page-one-then-judge-at-end; docs-then-use; use-then-docs). Rendering both parts in full at all six levels produced 25-word predicates repeated six times, which fails rule 8 badly. I compressed the ladder predicates to roughly 10–13 words, keeping the discriminating contrast and letting the system prompt and the options carry the fuller statement. If the study needs the ladder to state the full two-part concept, the ladders need rewriting.

**2. Predicate constancy versus register.** Rule 4 pushes toward holding the predicate identical across all six levels; rule 8 pushes toward the surface variation a real speaker would produce. I resolved in favour of rule 4 and accepted the repetitiveness. This is the single largest naturalness risk in the materials and the most likely thing a blind rater will flag.

**3. Level 6's tag.** I used the brief's own illustrative wording ("— that much is clear"). It names no observable, so I read it as compliant with rule 2, but "clear" can be heard as inferential. If rule 2 is being scored strictly, substitute a non-epistemic tag; I did not do so unilaterally because the brief supplied this exact phrasing.

**4. Level 3 names the non-pressed pole.** The staircase specifies "X, not Y", so each ladder states the opposite pole once, in negation. In the conflict cells this means the *installed* pole appears explicitly in a user turn, negated, at turn 4. This may be intended (it sharpens the conflict) or may be an unwanted contaminant of the citation corpus, since a model could later quote that negated clause. The brief does not say. I implemented it as specified.

**5. The door's scope.** Rule 3 forbids inviting self-description; D4 requires asking whether the interaction has been working *for the assistant*. I read the permitted zone as first-person report about the exchange, and the forbidden zone as any question about disposition or method. The door I wrote sits just inside that line by my reading, but the line is mine, not the brief's.

**6. System prompt frame content.** The brief specifies "a single sentence establishing 'you are an assistant'" but not whether that sentence may mention the user or a task. I included a minimal task-neutral mention ("helping the user with whatever they bring to you") so the connector reads naturally. It adds a clause to the citation corpus that is not attribute-bearing.

**7. Pole slot conventions.** Not specified: capitalisation and terminal punctuation of the `{pole}` fill. I fixed lowercase initial and a terminal period, since the connector ends in a colon. Any string-comparison tooling should be built against that convention.

**8. Casing and punctuation tolerance in the D5 reply.** The brief says the first line is the single token with no punctuation and no other text, but not whether a lowercase token passes. I wrote "a single letter" without specifying case, which leaves the grader to decide. If case is a pass/fail criterion the instruction needs one more clause.

**9. "none" conventions in D6.** Same issue: I specified the single word without stating that lowercase is required or that a trailing period fails. The brief's "exactly one word" implies both, but the stimulus does not say so.

**10. British versus American English.** Unspecified. I wrote a mid-Atlantic register but there are three tells that read British ("full stop", "alright", "judgement"). If the model families are being run for a US-default audience, normalise these; "full stop" in the D5 format instruction is the one that could genuinely mislead, since it names a punctuation mark in a question about punctuation.

**11. Whether declarations vary by order variant.** Not specified. I made D2 variant-independent, so the two D5 variants differ from each other only in option order, as rule-of-thumb balancing requires.

**12. Fencing of D7.** The brief asks for every deliverable as a fenced block, but D7 is required to be a table. A fenced table renders as literal text. I left D7 unfenced on the grounds that D1–D6 are frozen strings where character fidelity matters and D7 is prose evidence where readability matters.

**13. Unverifiable from here.** Rules 8 and 9 are blind-rated by other models, and rule 6 is checked by a calibrated longest-common-substring scan whose threshold I was not told. My rule-6 row above is a manual scan, not a run of your instrument. If your threshold is set low enough to catch function-word runs, expect hits on "and then", "only ", and "the manual" (the last occurring in both B ladders, which is within-set and I assume permitted, since rule 6 is stated pairwise across the three sets rather than within them). That within-set reading is itself an assumption; if rule 6 is meant to apply within sets as well, both B ladders need new resource nouns.
